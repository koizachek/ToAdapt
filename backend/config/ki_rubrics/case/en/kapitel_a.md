# 2 Chapter A

Three friends, a running shoe and the question of how far you can run.

## 2.1 The founding

In January 2010, three people meet in a café in Zurich. Olivier Bernhard has an obsessive idea of how the perfect running shoe must feel: soft landing, explosive push-off. David Allemann understands brands: how to build them, how to protect them, and how quickly they can be destroyed. Caspar Coppetti thinks in business models and scaling logics.

What unites them is the conviction that the running shoe market is ripe for disruption. Nike, Adidas and Asics dominate with technologies that have been based on the same principle for decades: thick foams that absorb impact energy. More cushioning means more material, more weight, more inertia. Bernhard's idea reverses the principle: a patented sole technology called CloudTec, in which hollow “Cloud elements” under the foot compress and spring back individually (On Holding AG, 2020b). The result is paradoxical: a shoe that lands softly and pushes off hard at the same time. In a running shoe industry that thinks in either-or categories, that breaks a taboo.

The first collection consists of a single model, the Cloudsurfer (On Holding AG, 2021d). They sell it through specialist running stores in Switzerland, with a story that tells itself: a former professional athlete has developed the shoe he always wished he had. The feedback is overwhelming. Runners report a running sensation they have never experienced before. Within a short time the shoe is available in several countries, not because ON has a distribution strategy, but because specialist retailers actively ask for it.

ON perspective: founding phase Olivier Bernhard had first offered his shoe idea to Nike and other major brands and had been turned down repeatedly. Only then did he realize the project himself in 2010 with David Allemann and Caspar Coppetti; the first prototype consisted of pieces of garden hose glued under an outsole. The first customers were elite athletes such as marathon runner Tegla Loroupe, and early growth came through specialist running retail rather than advertising: by 2011, more than 200 specialist stores in more than 14 countries carried the brand. Bernhard's guiding principle: “We do the impossible. Everything that is possible we leave to the competition.” (our translation). ## 2.2 From niche product to global player

What happens between 2010 and 2025 is one of the most unusual growth stories in the European sporting goods industry. ON grows from a three-person start-up into a listed company with more than 3'000 employees, sales of over CHF 3 billion and a presence in more than 90 countries. What is remarkable is not only the pace but the kind of growth: ON has grown with positive operating profitability (adjusted EBITDA), despite net losses in 2019 to 2021, without ever fighting major discount battles or giving up its premium positioning.[^2]

The growth can be described in four phases, each of which brought its own management challenges:

In the niche phase (2010–2014), ON concentrated exclusively on specialist running shoes for ambitious runners. Distribution ran through specialist retail, and the brand was built through word of mouth and local running events. The organization was small, decision paths were short, and everyone knew everyone. The first international expansion to Germany (2011) and to the USA and Japan (2013) came about not through strategic planning but through the founders' personal contacts with specialist retailers in these markets.[^3]

The broadening phase (2014–2018) marked the transition from a pure running specialist to a broader sports shoe provider. ON launched models for everyday use: shoes that were good enough for running and attractive enough for the city. This step was controversial internally: purists on the team feared the brand would lose its credibility with runners. But the bet paid off. The lifestyle models opened up a new target group without driving away the existing one. In parallel, ON began building up the North American market, which would prove to be a game changer.

In the scaling phase (2018–2021), ON built its own digital platform and invested massively in direct sales. The partnership with Roger Federer, who joined in November 2019 as co-entrepreneur and investor, catapulted the brand into a new category of attention. Federer was not only an ambassador but the co-developer of “The Roger” line, whose first model was released in 2020 (On Holding AG, 2019, 2020c, 2021a). The IPO on the New York Stock Exchange in September 2021 was the symbolic conclusion of this phase: a valuation of around USD 7 billion at the issue price of USD 24; on the first day of trading the share closed 46 percent higher, corresponding to a market value of around USD 11 billion (On Holding AG, 2021c; Handelsblatt, 2021; SRF, 2021).

The current transformation phase (2021–today) is marked by a shift in the guiding question. Where it used to be “How do we grow?”, the question now is: “How do we grow without losing what makes us who we are?” ON is expanding into apparel and accessories, building its own retail stores, developing the Chinese market and at the same time trying to preserve the start-up culture that shaped the company. Whether that succeeds is open.

## 2.3 What ON does differently

Comparing ON with the established players, four dimensions stand out that together form an unusual combination. None of them is unique on its own, but in combination they create a brand positioning that is hard to replicate.

The first dimension is technology as the brand core. CloudTec is not one feature among many but the identity of the company. Every ON shoe is based on the same underlying technology in ever new variations, from the Cloudboom racing shoe to the everyday Cloud model. That creates recognizability and a consistent brand experience, comparable to Apple's design language. But it is also a dependency: if a competitor develops a similar technology or the patents expire, ON loses its technological unique selling point. The relevant question is whether the brand will then be strong enough to carry the company on its own.

The second dimension is Swiss design as differentiation. The minimalist, understated design is deliberately different from the loud, color-intensive products of Nike or Adidas. ON shoes are instantly recognizable on the street, not through a conspicuous logo but through the characteristic sole structure and the restrained color palette. This aesthetic attracts a specific target group: design-minded, well-earning, urban people between 25 and 45 who value understatement. The flip side: this target group is lucrative but limited. Growth beyond this segment requires either an extension of the design language or an acceptance of the niche.

The third dimension is community instead of advertising. ON has invested comparatively little in classic advertising. Instead, the company relied on community building: local running groups, events, social media and high-profile partnerships. The collaboration with Roger Federer is the most visible element, but not the only one. In more than 30 cities, ON organizes regular running meet-ups where the brand can be experienced without any sales pitch. This strategy is more authentic than classic advertising, but it scales more slowly.

[^4][^5]The fourth dimension is DTC as a strategic pillar. ON is systematically expanding its direct sales: its own website, app and, since late 2019, its own stores: at the end of 2024 there were 19 flagship and retail stores, including Zurich, New York, Tokyo, London and Paris, plus 30 smaller mall stores in China; at the end of 2025 ON counted just under 70 own locations. The DTC share of sales rose from just under 38% in 2020 to around 41% in 2024 and 42% in 2025, with absolute DTC growth of around 40% per year (2024 as reported, 2025 in constant currency; 2025 as reported +33.7%). This improves margins considerably, gives ON control over the brand experience and delivers valuable customer data. At the same time it creates tensions with the specialist retailers that made ON big in the early years. This tension is not incidental; it is one of the central management questions ON has to answer in the coming years.

## 2.4 The market ON operates in

The global sports shoe market has a volume of around USD 150 billion and grows at around 5% per year, driven by athleisure trends, growing health awareness and urban lifestyles. But growth is unevenly distributed, both geographically and by segment.[^6]

The Americas region, essentially North America, is ON's largest market with around 64% of sales. This is where ON made its breakthrough, especially with a target group that finds Nike too ubiquitous and Adidas too fashion-driven. The market keeps growing, but competition is intensifying: Nike, New Balance and Hoka are fighting for exactly the same premium running target group. ON's advantage in North America is its perception as the “European alternative”, a positioning that becomes harder to sustain as awareness grows.

Europe (EMEA) is the home market with around 25% of sales. ON has been present here since its founding and is firmly rooted in the running scene. Growth increasingly comes from new categories (apparel, lifestyle, outdoor) and less from the core running shoe product, which is reaching saturation in many European markets. Specialist retail relationships are particularly important in Europe: runners here traditionally buy in specialist stores, not online. That makes the DTC expansion politically more delicate in Europe than in North America.

[^7][^8]Asia-Pacific is the growth market, with only 11% of sales in 2024 but growth of 85% over the previous year; in 2025 the share was already 17%. China is the big prize and at the same time the big unknown. ON has been present there since 2018, runs its own subsidiary in Shanghai, operated around 30 smaller mall stores at the end of 2024 and sells via the platforms Tmall and JD.com. But the brand is hardly known, competition with local players such as Anta and Li-Ning is intense, and consumers have different expectations of brands than in Europe or North America. The internal assessment swings between “strategically indispensable” and “money pit”.

## 2.5 What keeps ON busy

In conversations with managers, employees and retail partners, the same topics keep coming up, not as neatly delineated problems but as recurring points of friction that reinforce one another. The following observations are not a complete analysis but starting points from which, on closer inspection, differently weighted challenges can emerge. The internal figures, retailer reactions and quotations in this section and in section 2.6 are case assumptions.

Speed and culture

In 15 years, ON has gone from a niche provider to a billion-franc company. The pace raises a fundamental question that many fast-growing companies know but few answer successfully: can ON preserve the culture of a start-up (flat hierarchies, short decision paths, founders' instinct) while at the same time building the structures of a corporation? Headcount has more than doubled since the IPO. Many of the new managers come from Nike, Adidas and McKinsey. They bring expertise, but also a different way of thinking. Long-serving employees report that decisions that used to be made over a coffee break now go through three rounds of alignment.

Who sells ON?

Expanding direct sales is financially attractive: higher margins, customer data, control over the brand experience. But it threatens the relationship with the specialist retailers that made ON big in the early years. In Europe, more than 60% of ON customers still buy in physical stores. If ON pushes DTC too aggressively, it risks losing exactly the retailers that built the brand. Some specialist retailers in Switzerland and Germany have already signaled that they will reduce ON shelf space if the DTC channel keeps growing. One Swiss specialist retailer put it this way: “We made ON popular, and now ON is taking our customers away.”

How broad may the brand become?

ON positions itself as a premium brand. List prices are noticeably above those of comparable models from Nike, Hoka or New Balance (see Exhibit C1). That works as long as the brand is perceived as exclusive and innovative. But the expansion into large chains such as Foot Locker and JD Sports (Axios Pro, 2022) and the entry into broader customer segments (lifestyle rather than running only) potentially dilute the exclusivity. The internal debate is heated: the sales team argues that every new point of sale brings revenue. The marketing team warns that every new point of sale makes the brand a bit more interchangeable. Both are right, and that is exactly what makes the decision so difficult.

Growth and responsibility

ON has set itself ambitious sustainability targets: science-based climate targets for 2030 (minus 42% absolute emissions in Scope 1 and 2 versus 2023, minus 51.6% Scope 3 emissions per unit produced), a rising share of recycled and renewable materials and, with “Cyclon”, a circular program in which the shoe is returned and recycled after use (On Holding AG, 2026c). At the same time, rapid growth requires more production, more logistics, more material consumption. The CO₂ footprint per shoe has fallen by 8% since 2022, but absolute CO₂ emissions have risen by 22% due to volume growth. The question of whether ON can grow and reduce its ecological footprint at the same time is not rhetorical; it is one of the hardest strategic questions the company has to answer.[^9]

## 2.6 Stakeholders with different expectations

ON operates in a complex web of stakeholders whose expectations partly coincide and partly contradict each other. Understanding these expectations is not only academically relevant: it determines which strategic options are realistic and which will fail against reality.

Since the IPO in 2021, ON has been exposed to the expectations of investors and shareholders who demand high growth with rising profitability. The adjusted EBITDA margin was 16.7% in 2024 and 18.8% in 2025 (On Holding AG, 2025b, 2026d): respectable for a growing company, but still below what the capital market is used to from established sporting goods groups. Analysts regularly point out that ON must improve its operational efficiency to survive as an independent company in the long term. The share price reacts sensitively to every miss of the growth forecast.

[^10]The workforce has grown in three years from around 1'200 (end of 2021) to around 3'250 (end of 2024). That creates a two-class society: the “old guard”, who experienced ON as a start-up and defend the founder culture, and the “newcomers”, who expect clearer structures, defined career paths and process reliability. Staff turnover was 18% in 2024, four percentage points above the industry average. In an internal survey, 34% of employees said they were “not sure” whether ON can preserve its culture as it grows.

Specialist retailers were ON's first distribution partners and built the brand. They expect exclusivity, fair terms and no cannibalization by ON's own stores and web shop. The growing DTC share is causing unrest. In Barcelona, a long-standing retail partner did not renew its contract, officially because of a “changed assortment strategy”, unofficially because of the feeling of being bypassed by ON.

ON's customer base has changed fundamentally. It used to be ambitious runners looking for technical shoes: people who did not care about price as long as the performance was right. Today it is increasingly design-oriented lifestyle buyers who perceive ON as a fashion brand. These two segments have different expectations of product, price and communication, and what excites one segment can alienate the other.

[^11]ON has its shoes manufactured entirely externally, around 90% in Vietnam and 10% in Indonesia, and works with fewer than 30 production partners across all products. The relationship is collaborative but strained: ON demands high quality standards and short lead times, while suppliers struggle with rising labor costs, raw material prices and tighter environmental regulations. The EU supply chain directive, whose application is scheduled from July 2029 after the 2026 revision, does not cover ON directly given the raised thresholds, but it raises expectations of transparency and responsibility in the supply chain via retail partners and customers.[^12]

## 2.7 Three voices, one company

The following three voices are fictitious. They were written for this case and represent the forces pulling at ON. They do not reproduce statements by real people.

“We must not make the mistake of taking our own growth for granted. Every quarter in which we do not deliver costs us on the capital market. Investors expect 20% growth with rising margins. We can only achieve that if we accelerate DTC and take off in China. Specialist retail will have to adapt, or it will be replaced.” – Finance perspective

“If we carry on like this, in five years we will be Nike with a smaller logo. Our brand lives from being different: special, authentic, not available everywhere. Every Foot Locker store that sells ON is another step toward the mass market. We have to have the courage to say no, even if it costs sales in the short term.” – Brand perspective

“Both are right, and that is the problem. We have to grow because our investors expect it and because our employees need prospects. But we have to grow differently from the others. The question is not whether, but how, and at what speed.” – Founder perspective

## 2.8 What the numbers do not show

Some developments that appear only between the lines of the annual reports and that could be relevant for analyzing the starting situation:

In April 2025, Co-CEO Marc Maurer announced his resignation as of the end of June after twelve years at ON. Martin Hoffmann, previously Co-CEO and CFO, took over as sole CEO on July 1, 2025. Around ten months later, in March 2026, Hoffmann also announced his resignation. The founders David Allemann and Caspar Coppetti, who remains Co-Chairmen of the board, took over operational leadership as Co-CEOs as of May 1, 2026; Scott Maguire became President and COO (On Holding AG, 2025c, 2026e). The double leadership vacancy in quick succession has raised questions internally and on the capital market: can ON build professional leadership structures, or does the company remain dependent on its founders?

The Cyclon circular shoe, launched in 2022 as a subscription model in which customers rent, return and recycle the shoe, reached only 12'000 subscribers worldwide by the time the subscription was discontinued in early 2026. The internal expectation was 50'000. Whether the model came too early, is too complicated or fundamentally does not scale is disputed internally.[^13]

[^14]In China, ON invested around CHF 40 million in the first two years, on sales of CHF 80 million. The region is not yet profitable. Management calls it a “strategic investment”, but there are voices on the board demanding a faster payback.

Negotiations with three large North American sporting goods chains on expanding distribution are well advanced. The marketing team has raised internal concerns that this could dilute the brand. The sales team argues that the additional revenue is needed to meet investor expectations (Case assumption, constructed for teaching purposes).

[^15]ON's patent protection for the core CloudTec technology expires in key markets between 2027 and 2029. Competitors such as Hoka have established cushioning systems of their own. Whether ON can maintain its technological lead becomes a strategic question of survival in the coming years.
