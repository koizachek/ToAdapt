# 2 Chapter A

Three friends, a running shoe and the question of how far you can run.

## 2.1 The founding

In January 2010, three people who could hardly be more different meet in a café in Zurich. Olivier Bernhard, former Ironman champion and duathlete, has an obsessive idea of how the perfect running shoe must feel: soft landing, explosive push-off. David Allemann, former marketing director at a Swiss media group, understands brands: how to build them, how to protect them, and how quickly they can be destroyed. Caspar Coppetti, fresh from his MBA at INSEAD, thinks in terms of business models and scaling logic.
What unites them: the conviction that the running shoe market is ripe for disruption. Nike, Adidas and Asics dominate with technologies that have been based on the same principle for decades: thick foams that absorb impact energy. More cushioning means more material, more weight, more inertia. Bernhard's idea reverses the principle: a patented sole technology called CloudTec, in which hollow “Cloud elements” under the foot compress individually and spring back. The result is paradoxical: a shoe that lands softly and pushes off hard at the same time. In the running shoe industry, which thinks in either-or categories, this is a breach of taboo.
The first collection consists of a single model in three colours. They sell it through specialised running shops in Switzerland, with a story that tells itself: a former professional athlete has developed the shoe he always wanted. The feedback is overwhelming. Runners report a running sensation they have never experienced before. Within six months, the shoe is available in ten countries, not because ON has a sales strategy, but because specialist retailers actively ask for it.

## 2.2 From niche product to global player

What happens between 2010 and 2025 is one of the most unusual growth stories in the European sporting goods industry. ON grows from a three-person start-up into a listed company with more than 3,000 employees, revenue of more than 3 billion Swiss francs and a presence in more than 60 countries. What is remarkable is not only the pace but the nature of the growth: ON has grown profitably without ever waging major discount battles or giving up its premium positioning.
The growth can be described in four phases, each of which brought its own management challenges.
In the niche phase (2010–2014), ON focused exclusively on specialised running shoes for ambitious runners. Sales ran through specialist retail, and the brand was built through word of mouth and local running events. The organisation was small, decision paths were short, and everyone knew everyone. The first international expansion to Germany, Austria and Japan did not come about through strategic planning, but through the founders' personal contacts with specialist retailers in these markets.
The broadening phase (2014–2018) marked the transition from pure running specialist to broader sports shoe provider. ON launched models for everyday use: shoes that were good enough for running and attractive enough for the city. This step was controversial internally: purists in the team feared that the brand would lose its credibility with runners. But the gamble paid off. The lifestyle models opened up a new target group without driving away the existing one. In parallel, the build-up of the North American market began, which was to prove a game changer.
In the scaling phase (2018–2021), ON built its own digital platform and invested massively in direct-to-consumer sales. The partnership with Roger Federer, announced in 2019 and effective from 2020, catapulted the brand into a new category of attention. Federer was not only a testimonial but also an investor and co-developer of the lifestyle line “The Roger”. The IPO on the New York Stock Exchange in September 2021 was the symbolic conclusion of this phase: a valuation of around 7.3 billion USD on the first trading day.
The current transformation phase (2021–today) is characterised by a shift in the guiding question. Whereas previously the question was “How do we grow?”, it is now: “How do we grow without losing what makes us who we are?” ON is expanding into apparel and accessories, building its own retail stores, entering the Chinese market and at the same time trying to preserve the start-up culture that has shaped the company. Whether this will succeed remains open.

## 2.3 What ON does differently

When ON is compared with the established players, four dimensions stand out which together form an unusual combination. None of them is unique on its own, but in combination they create a brand positioning that is difficult to replicate.
The first dimension is technology as the core of the brand. CloudTec is not one feature among many, but the identity of the company. Every ON shoe is based on the same underlying technology, in ever new variations, from the Cloudboom racing shoe to the Cloud everyday model. This creates recognisability and a consistent brand experience, comparable to Apple's design language. But it is also a dependency: if a competitor develops a similar technology or the patents expire, ON loses its technological unique selling point. The relevant question is whether the brand will then be strong enough to carry the company on its own.
The second dimension is Swiss design as differentiation. The minimalist, understated design is deliberately different from the loud, colourful products of Nike or Adidas. ON shoes are instantly recognisable on the street, not by a conspicuous logo, but by the characteristic sole structure and the restrained colour palette. This aesthetic attracts a specific target group: design-conscious, well-earning, urban people between 25 and 45 who value understatement. The flip side: this target group is lucrative but limited. Growth beyond this segment requires either a broadening of the design language or an acceptance of the niche.
The third dimension is community instead of advertising. ON has invested comparatively little in classic advertising. Instead, the company relied on community building: local running groups, events, social media and prominent partnerships. The collaboration with Roger Federer is the most visible element, but not the only one. In more than 30 cities, ON organises regular running meet-ups at which the brand can be experienced without any sales conversation taking place. This strategy is more authentic than classic advertising, but it scales more slowly.
The fourth dimension is DTC as a strategic pillar. ON is systematically expanding its direct-to-consumer sales: its own website, app and, since 2021, also physical stores, now at around 60 locations worldwide, including New York, London, Zurich, Paris, Shanghai and Tokyo. The DTC share of revenue rose from 22% in 2020 to around 41% in 2024. This improves margins considerably, gives ON control over the brand experience and provides valuable customer data. At the same time, it creates tensions with specialist retail, which made ON big in its early years. This tension is not incidental; it is one of the central management questions that ON must answer in the coming years.

## 2.4 The market in which ON operates

The global sports footwear market has a volume of around 150 billion USD and is growing at 5–7% per year, driven by athleisure trends, growing health awareness and urbanised lifestyles. But growth is unevenly distributed, both geographically and by segment.
North America is ON's largest market, with around 55% of revenue. This is where ON achieved its breakthrough, particularly with a target group that perceives Nike as too ubiquitous and Adidas as too fashion-driven. The market continues to grow, but competition is intensifying: Nike, New Balance and Hoka are fighting for exactly the same premium running target group. ON's advantage in North America is its perception as the “European alternative”, a positioning that becomes harder to maintain as awareness increases.
Europe is the home market, with around 30% of revenue. ON has been present here since its founding and is firmly anchored in the running scene. Growth increasingly comes from new categories (apparel, lifestyle, outdoor) and less from the core product, the running shoe, which is reaching its saturation point in many European markets. Relationships with specialist retail are particularly important in Europe: runners here traditionally buy in specialised shops, not online. This makes the DTC expansion in Europe politically more delicate than in North America.
Asia-Pacific is the growth market, currently accounting for only 12% of revenue, but with growth of 65% last year. China is both the big prize and the big unknown. ON has had an office there since 2022, first pop-up stores and a growing social media presence on WeChat and Xiaohongshu. But the brand is hardly known, competition with local players such as Anta and Li-Ning is intense, and consumers have different expectations of brands than in Europe or North America. The internal assessment fluctuates between “strategically indispensable” and “a billion-franc money pit”.

## 2.5 What preoccupies ON

In conversations with executives, employees and retail partners, the same topics come up again and again, not as neatly delineated problems but as recurring points of friction that reinforce each other. The following observations are not a complete analysis, but starting points from which, on closer examination, challenges of differing weight may emerge.
Speed and culture
In 15 years, ON has grown from a niche provider into a billion-franc company. The pace raises a fundamental question that many fast-growing companies know, but few answer successfully: can ON preserve the culture of a start-up (flat hierarchies, short decision paths, founders' intuition) while at the same time building the structures of a corporation? The number of employees has more than doubled since the IPO. Many of the new executives come from Nike, Adidas and McKinsey. They bring expertise, but also a different mindset. Long-standing employees report that decisions which used to be made during a coffee break now go through three rounds of alignment.
Who sells ON?
The expansion of direct-to-consumer sales is financially attractive: higher margins, customer data, control over the brand experience. But it threatens the relationship with specialist retail, which made ON big in its early years. In Europe, more than 60% of ON customers still buy in bricks-and-mortar retail. If ON bets too aggressively on DTC, it risks losing precisely the retailers who built the brand. Some specialist retailers in Switzerland and Germany have already signalled that they will reduce ON floor space if the DTC channel continues to grow. One Swiss specialist retailer put it this way: “We made ON popular, and now ON is taking our customers away.”
How broad can the brand become?
ON positions itself as a premium brand. Prices are 20–40% above the industry average. This works as long as the brand is perceived as exclusive and innovative. But the expansion into large chains such as Foot Locker and JD Sports and the entry into broader customer segments (lifestyle instead of just running) potentially dilute its exclusivity. The internal debate is heated: the sales team argues that every new point of sale brings revenue. The marketing team warns that every new point of sale makes the brand a little more interchangeable. Both are right, and that is exactly what makes the decision so difficult.
Growth and responsibility
ON has set itself ambitious sustainability goals: net zero by 2040, recyclable materials, the “Cyclon” line as a circular product, in which the shoe is returned after use and recycled. At the same time, rapid growth requires more production, more logistics, more material consumption. The CO₂ footprint per shoe has fallen by 8% since 2022, but absolute CO₂ emissions have risen by 22% due to volume growth. The question of whether ON can grow and at the same time reduce its ecological footprint is not rhetorical; it is one of the hardest strategic questions the company must answer.

## 2.6 Stakeholders with different expectations

ON operates in a complex web of stakeholder groups whose expectations are partly aligned and partly contradictory. Understanding these expectations is not only academically relevant: it determines which strategic options are realistic and which will fail in reality.
Since the IPO in 2021, ON has been exposed to the expectations of investors and shareholders, who demand high growth with rising profitability. The EBITDA margin in 2024 was around 17%: respectable for a growing company, but well below the level of Nike at 28%. Analysts regularly point out that ON must improve its operational efficiency in order to survive as an independent company in the long term. The share price reacts sensitively to every miss of the growth forecast.
The workforce has grown from 1,200 to more than 3,000 in three years. This creates a two-tier society: the “old guard”, who experienced ON as a start-up and defend the founders' culture, and the “newcomers”, who expect clearer structures, defined career paths and process certainty. Staff turnover in 2024 was 18%, four percentage points above the industry average. In an internal survey, 34% of employees stated that they were “not sure” whether ON can preserve its culture as it grows.
Specialist retailers were ON's first sales partners and built the brand. They expect exclusivity, fair terms and no cannibalisation by ON's own stores and web shop. The growing DTC share is causing unrest. In Barcelona, a long-standing retail partner did not renew its contract, officially because of a “changed assortment strategy”, unofficially because of the feeling of having been sidelined by ON.
ON's customer base has changed fundamentally. In the past, it consisted of ambitious runners looking for technical shoes: people who did not care about price as long as the performance was right. Today, it increasingly consists of design-oriented lifestyle buyers who perceive ON as a fashion brand. These two segments have different expectations of product, price and communication, and what delights one segment can alienate the other.
ON has its products manufactured by six suppliers in Vietnam and Indonesia. The relationship is a partnership, but a strained one: ON demands high quality standards and short delivery times, while the suppliers struggle with rising wage costs, raw material prices and stricter environmental regulations. The EU supply chain directive, which takes effect from 2027, will further increase the requirements for transparency and responsibility in the supply chain.

## 2.8 What the figures do not show

Some developments that appear in the annual reports only between the lines and that could be relevant for analysing the initial situation:
In April 2025, Co-CEO Marc Maurer announced his resignation after twelve years at ON. Martin Hoffmann, previously Co-CEO and CFO, took over the role of sole CEO. Only a few months later, Hoffmann also left the company. The founders David Allemann and Caspar Coppetti then returned to operational management as Co-CEOs. The double leadership vacancy in quick succession has raised questions internally and on the capital market: can ON build professional leadership structures, or will the company remain dependent on its founders?
The Cyclon circular shoe, a subscription model in which customers rent the shoe, return it and have it recycled, has so far only 12’000 subscribers worldwide. The internal expectation was 50’000. Whether the model came too early, is too complicated or fundamentally does not scale is disputed internally.
In China, ON invested around 40 million CHF in the first two years, with revenue of 80 million CHF. The region is not yet profitable. Management calls it a “strategic investment”, but there are voices on the board demanding faster payback.
Negotiations with three large North American sporting goods chains about an expansion of distribution are well advanced. The marketing team has internally expressed concerns that this could dilute the brand. The sales team argues that the additional revenue is necessary to meet investor expectations.
ON's patent protection for the CloudTec core technology expires in key markets between 2027 and 2029. Hoka has already brought a comparable cushioning system to market. The question of whether ON can maintain its technological lead will become an existential strategic question in the coming years.
