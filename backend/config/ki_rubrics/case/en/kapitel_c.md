# 4 Chapter C

Shaping market access: price, channel and positioning.

## 4.1 The pricing question

Note on deliberate simplification: In practice, product, price, communication and distribution influence one another; a different product portfolio changes the willingness to pay, a different positioning changes the pricing latitude. The case study and the assignments deliberately isolate the pricing decision and the channel decision so that deriving them from the segment data can be practised cleanly. The lecture shows the interactions between the marketing instruments in their full breadth; the consistency check in Touchpoint 3 and in the exam brings the decisions back together.
ON positions itself in the upper price segment of the running shoe market. This is no coincidence but a deliberate decision: premium prices signal quality and exclusivity, enable higher margins and protect the brand from the price war that characterises the mass market. But this positioning also creates vulnerability. The more expensive a product is, the higher customers' expectations are: of technology, design, service and experience. And the more broadly ON distributes, the more difficult it becomes to maintain the premium claim credibly.
The decisive question for the pricing strategy is how different customer segments respond to price changes. To this end, ON conducted a price test in selected markets in the third quarter of 2024, simulating a 10 per cent price increase. ## 4.2 The channel question

A pricing decision only takes effect through the channel in which it is implemented. And here ON has changed considerably within a few years: the company grew large through specialist retail: it was running shops with advisory expertise that made ON credible in its early phase. Today, 41 per cent of revenue comes from direct-to-consumer sales via its own online shop and its own stores, with growth of 40 per cent compared with the previous year.
The appeal of direct-to-consumer sales is obvious. The gross margin is around 60 per cent instead of 40 to 45 per cent in retail, because the retail margin is eliminated. ON determines price, presentation and availability itself; a discounted product in a large-format store undermines the premium position, whereas this does not happen in its own shop. And ON sees who buys: size, purchase history, return behaviour, response to campaigns. These data are completely absent in the wholesale business.
The price for this is reach and credibility. Specialist retail brings ON to customers who are not specifically looking for the brand, and it provides the advice through which performance products can be explained in the first place. The large chains open up access to the mass market and are growing at 22 per cent. The company's own stores, in turn, are not yet profitable despite their high gross margin (break-even is expected for 2026), because rent and staff costs are only covered above a certain volume. Direct-to-consumer sales are therefore not automatically the better channel, but rather the more expensive build-up with the higher return afterwards.
The question arises most sharply with third-party platforms. They account for 10 per cent of revenue at the lowest margin, provide no customer data and are the environment in which discount campaigns are least controllable. At the same time, in some markets their reach is the fastest route to new customer groups.
Internally, there is therefore a discussion about how far direct-to-consumer sales should be expanded and whether this can apply equally to all product categories and segments.

## 4.3 Communication and brand positioning

ON's communication strategy has changed in parallel with its growth. In the early phase, it was almost exclusively community-driven: local running events, word of mouth, personal recommendations from specialist retailers. With the IPO and the international expansion, marketing was professionalised and diversified.
The marketing budget in 2024 was around 320 million CHF, roughly 14 per cent of revenue. Of this, 48 per cent goes to social media, 22 per cent to events and sponsoring, 18 per cent to DTC marketing and 12 per cent to traditional advertising. The 2024 Zendaya campaign, which positioned ON as a lifestyle brand, was the most expensive single measure in the company's history, and internally the most controversial. The running community reacted with mixed feelings: some welcomed the increase in brand awareness, others saw it as proof that ON is losing its roots.
The internal debate can be boiled down to one question: How much lifestyle can the brand take before it loses its credibility with its core customers? The lifestyle segment is growing fastest and generates the highest margins in the DTC channel. But the brand identity is based on running and performance. If the perception tips, from "innovative running shoe brand that also looks good" to "fashionable lifestyle brand that also makes running shoes", ON could lose the trust of both customer segments: the runners, because the brand is no longer "theirs", and the lifestyle buyers, because the performance heritage was the only reason to pay more.
International distribution as a special case
In markets without its own DTC business, such as South-East Asia and the Gulf region, ON works with local distributors. There, the familiar pattern is reversed: the brand achieves its highest end-customer prices precisely in the channels that ON controls least. The distributors determine pricing, placement and assortment, and the customer data remain entirely with them; two partners are now demanding multi-year exclusive rights in return for guaranteed purchase volumes. Management is divided over whether this exchange (reach and price premium in return for control) supports or undermines the premium positioning.

## 4.4 The question of consistency

Price, channel and positioning are not three separate decisions, but three sides of the same one. A premium price cannot be sustained in a discount-driven platform environment. A positioning as a performance brand loses credibility if specialist retail disappears as the channel that explains the products. And while expanding the lifestyle segment does increase the margin in direct-to-consumer sales, it shifts precisely the perception on which the core customers' willingness to pay is based.
The question is therefore not only which decision is best in each individual area, but whether the three together form a consistent picture.
