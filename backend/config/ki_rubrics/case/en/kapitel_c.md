Shaping market access: price, channel and positioning.

## 4.1 The pricing question

Note on the deliberate simplification: in practice, product, price, communication and distribution influence one another; a different product portfolio changes willingness to pay, a different positioning changes pricing latitude. The case study and the assignments deliberately isolate the pricing and channel decisions so that the derivation from the segment data can be practiced cleanly. The lecture shows the interactions between the marketing instruments in full breadth; the coherence check in Touchpoint 3 and in the exam brings the decisions back together.

ON positions itself in the upper price segment of the running shoe market. That is no accident but a deliberate decision: premium prices signal quality and exclusivity, enable higher margins and protect the brand from the price war that characterizes the mass market. But this positioning also creates vulnerability. The more expensive a product, the higher customers' expectations: of technology, design, service and experience. And the more broadly ON distributes, the harder it becomes to credibly uphold the premium claim.

The decisive question for the pricing strategy is how different customer segments react to price changes. In the third quarter of 2024, ON ran a price test in selected markets simulating a 10% price increase. The results show considerable differences between the customer segments:

## 4.2 The channel question

A pricing decision only takes effect through the channel in which it is enforced. And here ON has changed a great deal within a few years: the company grew big through specialist retail; it was running stores with advisory expertise that made ON credible in the early phase. Today, 41% of sales come from direct sales via its own online shop and its own stores, with growth of 40% over the previous year (On Holding AG, 2025a, 2025b).

[^20]The appeal of direct sales is obvious. The gross margin is around 60% instead of 40 to 45% in retail, because the retail margin disappears. ON determines price, presentation and availability itself; a discounted product in a big-box store undermines the premium position, in its own shop that does not happen. And ON sees who buys: size, purchase history, return behavior, response to campaigns. This data is entirely missing in the wholesale business.

The price for that is reach and credibility. Specialist retail brings ON to customers who are not specifically looking for the brand, and it provides the advice through which performance products are explained in the first place. The large chains open access to the mass market and are growing at 22%. The company's own stores, in turn, are not yet profitable despite the high gross margin (break-even is expected for 2026), because rents and staff are only covered above a certain volume. Direct sales are therefore not automatically the better channel, but the more expensive build-up with the higher return afterwards.

The question is sharpest for third-party platforms. They account for 10% of sales at the lowest margin, deliver no customer data and are the environment in which discount campaigns are least controllable. At the same time, their reach is the fastest route to new customer groups in some markets.

Internally, therefore, the discussion is how far direct sales should be expanded and whether that can apply equally to all product categories and segments.

## 4.3 Communication and brand positioning

ON's communication strategy has changed in parallel with its growth. In the early phase it was almost entirely community-driven: local running events, word of mouth, personal recommendations by specialist retailers. With the IPO and international expansion, marketing was professionalized and diversified.

[^21][^22]The marketing budget in 2024 was around CHF 320 million, about 14% of sales. Of this, 48% goes to social media, 22% to events and sponsoring, 18% to DTC marketing and 12% to classic advertising. The Zendaya campaign of 2024, which positioned ON as a lifestyle brand, was the most expensive single measure in the company's history, and internally the most controversial. The running community reacted with mixed feelings: some welcomed the boost in awareness, others saw it as proof that ON is losing its roots.

The internal debate can be boiled down to one question: how much lifestyle can the brand take before it loses its credibility with its core customers? The lifestyle segment is growing fastest and generates the highest margins in the DTC channel. But the brand identity is based on running and performance. If the perception tips from “innovative running shoe brand that also looks good” to “fashionable lifestyle brand that also makes running shoes”, ON could lose the trust of both customer segments: the runners, because the brand is no longer “theirs”, and the lifestyle buyers, because the performance origin was the only reason to pay more.

International distribution as a special case

In markets without its own DTC business, such as Southeast Asia and the Gulf region, ON works with local distributors. There, a reversal of the familiar pattern appears: the brand achieves its highest retail prices in exactly the channels ON controls least. The distributors determine pricing, placement and assortment, and the customer data remains entirely with them; two partners are now demanding multi-year exclusivity rights in exchange for guaranteed purchase volumes. Management is divided on whether this trade (reach and price premium for control) supports or erodes the premium positioning.

## 4.4 The question of coherence

Price, channel and positioning are not three separate decisions but three sides of the same one. A premium price cannot be held in a discount-driven platform environment. A positioning as a performance brand loses credibility if specialist retail drops out as the explanatory channel. And an expansion of the lifestyle segment raises the margin in direct sales, but shifts exactly the perception on which the core customers' willingness to pay rests.

The question is therefore not only which decision is best in each individual field, but whether the three together form a coherent picture.
