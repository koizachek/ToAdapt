## 6.1 What holds ON together and what pulls it apart

Different observers describe ON differently. For investors it is a fast-growing DTC company with a strong margin. For runners it is a technology brand. For lifestyle buyers it is a design label. For specialist retail it is an increasingly difficult partner. None of these perspectives is wrong, but they show that ON does not present a uniform picture. The question of what holds ON together at its core becomes more urgent with every step of growth.

Three observations that may help you form your own assessment:

ON does not just sell shoes but a promise: that performance and aesthetics are not a contradiction. This promise is the core of the brand. It works as long as the performance side remains credible. If ON is perceived primarily as a fashion brand, the promise loses its foundation.

The value proposition is based on a combination that no competitor offers in this form: patented running technology that delivers a measurable performance advantage, packaged in a design so good that people wear the shoe even without an athletic occasion. The Swiss origin and the community orientation reinforce the brand perception.

The value creation logic follows an asset-light model: product development and brand stay in-house, production is outsourced to specialized suppliers. The growing DTC share shifts value creation from the pure product level to the experience level: ON sells not just shoes but a brand experience controlled via website, app, stores and events. The technology platform is the infrastructure that makes this experience scalable.

[^27]The revenue model is based on premium pricing with a high gross margin. In DTC the margin is around 60%, in wholesale 45%. Growth comes primarily from volume and category expansion, not from price increases. The Cyclon subscription model is an experiment meant to show whether circular business models work in the sporting goods industry, so far without resounding success.

The question is how stable this model is. Three factors could destabilize it: the patent expiry, which threatens technological differentiation; the dependence on a few suppliers, which creates quality and delivery risks; and the tension between volume and exclusivity, which undermines the premium positioning if it is not actively managed.

## 6.2 How ON is changing

Anyone comparing ON in 2019 and ON in 2025 sees a fundamentally different company. Not only in size, but in the way decisions are made, responsibilities distributed and conflicts fought out. The following observations come from interviews with employees at various levels.

As far as strategic direction is concerned, ON is making a shift from growth-driven to profitability-conscious management. The “growth at all costs” phase that made the IPO possible is giving way to a more differentiated question: which growth is worthwhile, and which costs more than it brings? This shift is intended by the board, but has not yet arrived in all parts of the organization.

Structurally, ON is changing from a flat start-up organization into a matrix with regional and functional responsibilities. New functions such as Chief Digital Officer and Head of Sustainability have only been filled since 2023. The number of management levels has grown from three to five since the IPO. That creates friction between those who value clarity and defined responsibilities and those who miss the agility of the earlier structure.

Most sensitive is what long-serving employees describe as cultural change. The founder culture (fast, bold, informal) increasingly collides with the process logic of a listed organization that demands compliance, reporting and governance. Many long-serving employees experience the cultural change as a loss. The employee survey shows: in 2022, 72% agreed with the statement that ON preserves its founder culture; in 2024 it is only 51%.

At the same time, standardization is advancing, especially in supply chain, quality management and compliance. That is necessary to work consistently with 3'250 employees at 14 locations. But every standardization is a trade-off: it increases efficiency and reduces agility. The question of how much standardization the speed of innovation can tolerate is one of the organization's core questions.

## 6.3 A letter from the executive team (fictitious)

The following letter is fictitious. It was written for this case, condenses positions that management has expressed publicly, and does not reproduce any statement by a real person. The letter is intensely discussed internally: some find it courageous, others worrying. Excerpts:

“Dear ON family, In 15 years we have built something nobody thought possible. From three people with an idea to a team of more than 3'000 that is changing the way people run and move. But I want to be honest: we are at a point where we have to decide what kind of company we want to be. Not what we sell; we know that. But how we work, how we decide, and what we are prepared to give up in order to preserve what makes us who we are. I see three things that concern me: first, speed. We make decisions more slowly than we used to, not because we have become less smart, but because more people have a say. That is inevitable, but we have to be careful not to lose our greatest strength: being faster than the big players. Second, identity. I am asked more and more often: is ON a running shoe brand or a lifestyle brand? My answer: we are a performance brand designed so well that people want to wear it outside of sport too. That order matters. Third, responsibility. We promised to be different from the others. More sustainable, more transparent, more human. Keeping that promise gets harder the bigger we become. But it is not negotiable.”

## 6.4 Open questions without answers

This case does not end with a solution. It ends with questions that ON itself has not yet answered and that may have no clear answer:

Which development in ON's environment has management underestimated the most so far, and what consequences does that have for the organization? Can ON remain a premium brand and at the same time expand into the mass market? Is DTC the right focus when specialist retail still reaches more than 60% of customers? How long does technology carry as differentiation when the patents expire? Can ON grow and at the same time reduce its ecological footprint? What happens to the culture if one day the founders withdraw from operational leadership, and who or what takes the place of the founders' vision as orientation? How would ON have to change its internal structures and processes to actually implement the chosen strategy?

These questions have no right answers. But the ability to think them through in a structured way, to decide on a sound basis and to critically reflect on your own decisions: that is the competence you train this semester.
