## 6.1 What Holds ON Together and What Pulls It Apart

Different observers describe ON in different ways. For investors, it is a fast-growing DTC company with a strong margin. For runners, it is a technology brand. For lifestyle buyers, it is a design label. For specialist retailers, it is an increasingly difficult partner. None of these perspectives is wrong, but they show that ON does not present a uniform picture. The question of what holds ON together at its core becomes more pressing with every step of growth.
Three observations that might be helpful for your own assessment:
ON sells not just shoes, but a promise: that performance and aesthetics are not a contradiction. This promise is the core of the brand. It works as long as the performance side remains credible. If ON is perceived primarily as a fashion brand, the promise loses its foundation.
The value proposition is based on a combination that no competitor offers in this form: patented running technology that provides a measurable performance advantage, packaged in a design so good that people wear the shoe even without a sporting occasion. The Swiss origin and the community orientation reinforce brand perception. 
The value creation logic follows an asset-light model: product development and brand remain in-house, while production is outsourced to specialised suppliers. The growing DTC share shifts value creation from the pure product level to the experience level: ON sells not just shoes, but a brand experience that is controlled via the website, app, stores and events. The technology platform is the infrastructure that makes this experience scalable.
The revenue model is based on premium pricing with a high gross margin. For DTC, the margin is around 60%; for wholesale, it is 45%. Growth comes primarily from volume and category expansion, not from price increases. The Cyclon subscription model is an experiment intended to show whether circular business models work in the sporting goods industry, so far without resounding success.
The question is how stable this model is. Three factors could destabilise it: patent expiry, which threatens technological differentiation; the dependency on a small number of suppliers, which creates quality and delivery risks; and the tension between volume and exclusivity, which undermines the premium positioning if it is not actively managed.

## 6.2 How ON Is Changing

Anyone comparing ON in 2019 with ON in 2025 sees a fundamentally different company. Not only in size, but in the way decisions are made, responsibilities are allocated and conflicts are resolved. The following observations come from interviews with employees at various levels.
As far as strategic direction is concerned, ON is making a shift from growth-driven to profitability-conscious management. The "growth at all costs" phase that made the IPO possible is giving way to a more nuanced question: which growth is worthwhile, and which costs more than it brings? This shift is intended by the Board, but has not yet reached all parts of the organisation.
Structurally, ON is changing from a flat start-up organisation into a matrix with regional and functional responsibilities. New functions such as Chief Digital Officer and Head of Sustainability have only been filled since 2023. The management hierarchy has expanded from three to five levels since the IPO. This creates friction between those who value clarity and defined responsibilities and those who miss the agility of the earlier structure.
The most sensitive issue is what long-standing employees describe as a cultural shift. The founder culture (fast, bold, informal) increasingly clashes with the process logic of a listed organisation that demands compliance, reporting and governance. Many long-standing employees experience the cultural change as a loss. The employee survey shows: in 2022, 72% agreed with the statement that ON preserves its founder culture; in 2024, only 51% did.
At the same time, standardisation is advancing, particularly in supply chain, quality management and compliance. This is necessary in order to work consistently with 3,200 employees at 14 locations. But every standardisation is a trade-off: it increases efficiency and reduces agility. The question of how much standardisation the speed of innovation can tolerate is one of the organisation's core questions.

## 6.4 Open Questions Without Answers

This case does not end with a solution. It ends with questions that ON itself has not yet answered and that may not have a clear answer:
Which development in ON's environment has management underestimated most so far, and what consequences does this have for the organisation? Can ON remain a premium brand while expanding into the mass market? Is DTC the right focus when specialist retail still reaches over 60% of customers? How long will technology sustain differentiation once the patents expire? Can ON grow and reduce its ecological footprint at the same time? What happens to the culture when the founder CEO steps down, and who or what takes the place of the founding vision as a source of orientation? How would ON have to change its internal structures and processes in order to actually implement the chosen strategy?
These questions have no right answers. But the ability to think them through in a structured way, to make well-founded decisions and to reflect critically on your own decisions: that is the competence you are training this semester.
