## 5.1 Supply Chain and Operations

ON's supply chain is spread across several continents and is becoming increasingly complex as the company grows. The company pursues an asset-light model: product development and brand management remain in-house, while production and logistics are outsourced to specialised partners. This keeps fixed costs low and enables rapid scaling, but it also creates dependencies and quality risks that increase with every new market and every new product category. Internally, however, there is discussion as to whether, in view of the growing DTC volumes and the rising return rate (currently 22% in the DTC channel), ON should build its own fulfilment centre: a decision that would change the entire cost structure.

## 5.2 Data as a Management Instrument and Its Blind Spots

With the expansion of direct-to-consumer sales, ON has gained something that is structurally missing in the wholesale business: it can see who buys. Size, purchase history, return behaviour, response to campaigns: all of this is generated in its own shop and in the app, and it is generated there automatically. For the past two years, there has therefore been internal discussion about how far demand, assortment and production planning can be managed on the basis of data.
The greatest lever lies in returns. In the online channel, more than one in five pairs is sent back, and the majority of these returns concern neither quality nor taste, but fit. A data-based size recommendation that learns from purchase history and reasons for return would have an immediate effect here: every return avoided saves logistics, inspection and refurbishment. The second lever lies in sales forecasting: ON makes production decisions six to nine months in advance, and forecast accuracy per model and market is well below what is considered achievable in the industry. Overly optimistic forecasts create inventories that later have to be sold off; overly cautious ones cost revenue in precisely the models that sell well.
The blind spot, however, is systematic and is underestimated internally. ON only sees the customers who buy directly, just under half of the business. About the majority, who buy from specialist retailers, the large chains or on platforms, the company knows nothing. Anyone who bases management on the available data is therefore optimising for the direct channel, and its customer base is not representative: it is younger, more digitally savvy and more strongly anchored in the lifestyle segment than the customer base as a whole. There is a possibility that, along this path, ON will drift away from precisely the customer group that keeps the brand technically credible, without the data ever pointing to it, because this group barely appears in them.

## 5.3 Sustainability: Ambition Meets Reality

ON has set itself ambitious sustainability targets: net zero by 2040 across all three scopes, 100% recyclable or bio-based materials by 2030, and the Cyclon programme as a flagship project for the circular economy. The ambitions are credible: ON invested in sustainable materials at an early stage and did not take up the issue only under public pressure.
The reality, however, is sobering. In 2024, only 34% of materials came from recycled or bio-based sources, far from the 100% target for 2030. The CO₂ footprint per shoe has fallen by 8% thanks to more efficient production, but absolute CO₂ emissions have risen by 22% due to volume growth. The Cyclon programme has 12,000 subscribers instead of the planned 50,000. The EU supply chain directive and the EU Digital Product Passport will impose concrete compliance requirements from 2026/2027 that will tie up additional resources.
