Who does what: supply chain, in-house activity and the limits of growth

## 5.1 Supply chain and operations

ON's supply chain is spread across several continents and becomes increasingly complex with growth. The company pursues an asset-light model: product development and brand management stay in-house, production and logistics are outsourced to specialized partners (On Holding AG, 2025a). That keeps fixed costs low and enables rapid scaling, but it also creates dependencies and quality risks that increase with every new market and every new product category. Internally, however, there is a debate about whether ON should build its own fulfillment center given the growing DTC volumes and the rising return rate (currently 22% in the DTC channel): a decision that would change the entire cost structure.

## 5.2 Data as a management tool and its blind spots

With the expansion of direct sales, ON has gained something structurally missing in the wholesale business: it sees who buys. Size, purchase history, return behavior, response to campaigns: all of this arises in its own shop and app, and it arises there automatically. Internally, therefore, there has been a two-year debate about how far demand, assortment and production planning can be managed on the basis of data.

The biggest lever lies in returns. In the online channel, more than one in five pairs comes back, and the majority of these returns concern neither quality nor taste but fit. A data-based size recommendation that learns from purchase history and return reasons would have an immediate effect here: every avoided return saves logistics, inspection and refurbishment. The second lever lies in sales forecasting: ON makes production decisions six to nine months in advance, and forecast accuracy per model and market is well below what is considered achievable in the industry. Overly optimistic forecasts create inventory that later has to be sold off; overly cautious ones cost sales in exactly the models that are selling.

The blind spot, however, is systematic and underestimated internally. ON only sees the customers who buy directly, just under half of the business. About the majority who buy in specialist retail, at the large chains or on platforms, the company knows nothing. Whoever bases management on the available data therefore optimizes for the direct channel, and its customers are not representative: they are younger, more digitally minded and more strongly anchored in the lifestyle segment than the customer base as a whole. There is a possibility that ON drifts away in this way from exactly the customer group that keeps the brand technically credible, without the data ever pointing to it, because this group hardly appears in them.

## 5.3 Sustainability: ambition meets reality

ON has set itself ambitious sustainability targets: climate targets validated by the Science Based Targets initiative (minus 42% absolute emissions in Scope 1 and 2 by 2030 versus 2023, minus 51.6% Scope 3 emissions per unit produced over the same period), material targets such as 20% recycled or renewable content in midsoles by 2027 and 100% renewable electricity at tier 1 suppliers by 2030, as well as the Cyclon program as a flagship project for circular business (On Holding AG, 2026c). The ambitions are credible: ON invested early in sustainable materials and did not take up the topic only under public pressure.

Reality, however, is sobering. In 2025, the share of preferred materials in the total product portfolio was 29.7%, and progress comes mainly from individual components: 23% recycled or renewable content in midsoles, 94% for polyester. The CO₂ footprint per shoe has fallen by 8% through more efficient production, but absolute CO₂ emissions have risen by 22% due to volume growth. The Cyclon subscription fell short of expectations with 12'000 instead of the planned 50'000 subscribers and was transferred in 2026 into a broader circular service program. The EU digital product passport (ESPR) schedules the delegated act for textiles indicatively for 2027; requirements apply only after its adoption. For footwear a study runs until the end of 2027; the EU supply chain directive follows from 2029. Both will tie up additional resources.[^23][^24][^25][^26]
