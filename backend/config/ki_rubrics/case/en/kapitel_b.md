# 3 Chapter B

Strategic crossroads: go broader or go deeper?

## 3.1 The strategic question

At the end of 2025, ON stands at a point where growth no longer happens automatically. The phase in which every new market and every new product generated sales almost by itself is coming to an end. The company has to decide deliberately where to direct its limited resources. Three strategic options are on the table, each with its own logic, its own risk profile and its own consequences for the organization.

The first option is geographic expansion, internally called “Global Push”: systematically building up China and Southeast Asia. That is where the largest volume growth lies, but also the highest risk. ON has no established brand awareness in Asia, no retail access via specialist stores, and has to compete against local players who know the market and consumer psychology better. The estimated investment is CHF 200 to 300 million over three years: money that would not be available for other priorities.

The second option is category expansion under the working title “Beyond Running”: building apparel, outdoor and lifestyle accessories as a second pillar. ON has already started with apparel, but apparel and accessories account for only around 7% of sales (2025), albeit with growth rates of more than 60% per year; the collection is still narrow and brand perception in textiles is not established. Full potential requires a dedicated textile supply chain, an expanded design team and a brand positioning that goes beyond shoes. The estimated investment is CHF 150 to 200 million.[^16]

The third option is a deepening under the working title “Premium Fortress”: penetrate existing markets more deeply, expand DTC, sharpen the premium positioning, accelerate innovation and deliberately keep distribution selective. Less volume growth, but higher margins and stronger brand control. The estimated investment is CHF 80 to 120 million, considerably less than the other options, but with the risk of not meeting investors' growth expectations.

The following voice is fictitious. The figures mentioned are case assumptions. “I hate to say it, but China is a money pit. We have invested 40 million and are not profitable. The brand is not known there, competition is brutal, and we do not understand the consumers. Every franc we put into China is missing from the DTC build-up in North America, where we demonstrably make money. I strongly recommend reducing the Asia investments to a minimum and deploying the resources where the return is visible.” – Member of the board of directors

This position directly contradicts the stance of the Head of Growth, who argues that in ten years ON will need the Chinese market to stay relevant. Who is right cannot be answered today, but the decision on how much ON invests in China has to be made now.

None of the three options completely rules out the others, but resources are limited. ON cannot do everything at once. Every prioritization creates winners and losers within the organization, and each has consequences for the relationship with stakeholders.

## 3.2 The competitive landscape

ON operates in a market dominated by a few large players, but one in which the balance of power is shifting. Nike, by far the largest company with USD 51 billion in sales, is in a growth crisis for the first time in decades. A misguided DTC strategy that reduced the wholesale channel too quickly and a CEO change in fall 2024 have unsettled the company. At the same time, rising brands such as Hoka and ON have gained market share.[^17]

The question is whether this shift is permanent or a temporary window that closes as soon as Nike recovers. Historically, Nike has always overcome crises: through innovation offensives, marketing power and sheer size. If Nike corrects its DTC strategy and at the same time invests in premium running, that could considerably narrow the space ON occupies.

## 3.3 ON's resource base: what is hard to copy?

For every strategic decision, the central question is what ON's competitive advantage rests on and how durable it is. The CloudTec technology is patent-protected, but the patents expire between 2027 and 2029. Hoka has already developed similar cushioning systems. Technology alone is not a sustainable advantage in the medium term; it has to be complemented by continuous innovation.[^18]

The brand and design DNA are harder to copy. ON's minimalist aesthetic and Swiss origin create a brand perception that cannot simply be replicated. New Balance attempts something similar with its “Made in USA” line, but the positioning is different. However, brands can be diluted, and that is exactly what threatens if ON distributes too broadly.

The DTC platform and customer data are growing and hard to replicate because they are built on proprietary data. The higher the DTC share, the more valuable this data treasure becomes, provided ON uses it systematically for personalization, product development and customer retention. Finally, the innovation culture and speed are perhaps ON's strongest but also most vulnerable advantage. ON brings new products to market faster than the big players: 18 months from idea to shelf, compared with 24 to 36 months at Nike. This agility is a direct function of organizational size. With every new employee, every additional layer of hierarchy and every further alignment process, it decreases.[^19]
